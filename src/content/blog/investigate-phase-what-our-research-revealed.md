## The problem, sharpened

During Engage, we described what students live. The investigation turned it into one sentence:

> Learners have access to content but not to understanding: when they are stuck, they cannot ask the best teachers a question, privately, at the moment they need it, in their own language — unless they can pay for it.

The figures behind it are hard to ignore. In Tunisia, 67% of families pay for private lessons — about 1,468 million TND a year (FTDES 2023). Worldwide, 2.2 billion people are still offline (ITU 2025). And on edX, course completion fell to about 3%.

## What we found

**The barrier is availability, not distance.** Video calls removed distance years ago; they never multiplied a teacher's time.

**The technology exists — for business.** Platforms like D-ID and Tavus already sell real-time talking avatars that answer from documents. But of the 26 tools we compared, none combines a mentor's face and voice with pedagogy, a model of what the student knows, or a link back to the real professor.

**Design decides whether AI helps.** In a large study, students using unguarded GPT-4 scored 17% lower on exams once the tool was removed — while a guardrailed tutor avoided the loss (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025).

**Trust is a requirement, not an option.** Since August 2026, users must be told they are talking to an AI; emotion recognition is banned in education; a professor's face and voice are biometric data that need explicit consent.

## Our objective tree

The synthesis organises everything into one tree: a main objective — *make the best knowledge available to every learner, wherever and whenever they are* — supported by three pillars and six multimodal modules: Voice & Audio, Language & Knowledge, Face & Video, Multilingual, Adaptive Personalization and Content Generation. Underneath, six enablers: consent and liveness check, a visible "This is an AI" label, teacher review and hand-off, a low-bandwidth mode, Derja and multimodal data, and data protection.

## What's next

Desk research is done; field work starts now. A student survey, interviews with professors, an AI expert, a lawyer and an educator, and seven experiments — including the one that matters most: an A/B test comparing a classic lecture video with an interactive avatar.
